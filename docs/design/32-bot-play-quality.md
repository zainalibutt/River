# Bot play-quality gate

This pass improves River's poker decisions separately from its offline opponent-action ML experiments. No trained model controls a live bot. The authoritative server still legalises each action and keeps the deterministic rule policy as its fallback. This document records local code and offline evidence, not a production deployment.

## Poker standard and scope

The old preflop score adds a large fixed pair bonus. As a result, a small pocket pair can outrank ace-king suited in the OG rule policy. This is a heuristic error, not a legitimate claim about hand equity. The existing flush-draw shortcut also sometimes treats an already-made hand as merely a nine-out draw. PokerStars' [starting-hand guide](https://www.pokerstars.com/poker/learn/lesson/poker-starting-hands/) distinguishes premium broadways from speculative small pairs, while its [pot-odds lesson](https://www.pokerstars.com/poker/learn/lesson/pot-odds/) distinguishes the immediate call price from drawing equity and implied odds. [Calculating outs](https://www.pokerstars.com/poker/learn/lesson/calculating-outs/) warns that apparent outs may not be clean. These are reference concepts, not proof that River's chosen score or thresholds are optimal.

The live default now wraps the old policy with one narrow OG-only correction. When the old policy would fold a made straight or better on a clean two-tone flop with a flush draw, or a made two pair or better on a clean two-tone turn with a flush draw, it calls if the immediate call share is at most 0.4 and calling is legal. It does not fire on paired or already three-suit boards. Rookie and Novice decisions are unchanged. Fixed scenarios prove the old fold and new call. In a 2,400-hand ordinary-bot audit this guard fired zero times and changed zero chips, so it is a targeted correctness fix, not demonstrated win-rate improvement.

## Broader OG preflop candidate, not live

`preflopHandStrengthV2` is a configurable ordinal ranking of hole cards, **not** a calibrated equity or win-probability estimate. It values premium broadways and high pairs above speculative small pairs and weak offsuit hands. The candidate also caps OG preflop bluff attempts, refuses an all-in response to a bet with a below-threshold hand, and avoids open-shoving a deep stack. Postflop behaviour remains the existing rule plus the made-hand draw guard. The default policy does **not** use this candidate.

The fixed 200-seed scenarios show the distinction: the legacy OG folded ace-king suited in more than 150 seeds while the candidate raised it in more than 150; the old policy raised pocket deuces in more than 80 seeds while the candidate folded in more than 150. The candidate also folds seven-deuce facing a raise, avoids open-shoving deep ace-king suited and never forced a seven-deuce all-in facing a shove in the 200-seed test. This is a correction to River's internal ordering, not evidence that ace-king should always raise or deuces should always fold. Position, stack depth, previous action, opponent ranges and multiway context all matter; PokerStars' [multiway guide](https://www.pokerstars.com/poker/learn/strategies/a-guide-to-multiway-pots/) explains why a wider heads-up range does not transfer unchanged to an eight-seat pot.

Before the bluff cap, a 1,200-hand mixed-eight audit found 124 candidate preflop all-ins, including junk hands facing large bets. After the cap and threshold, the same audit found 17, with no sampled deep-stack open shove and no sampled junk hand. The direct junk-versus-shove test is the stronger deterministic gate; the audit is a sample, not a guarantee that every possible hand is sound.

## Reproducible outcome checks

`bot-preflop-audit.ts` compares the live guard baseline and the candidate on the same deck, with the focal OG policy as the only changed seat. Its latest 1,600-hand heads-up run was +68.97 big blinds per 100 hands for the candidate. Its 1,200-hand mixed-eight run was -337.43, and tough-eight was +10.92. Every individual approximate 95% interval crossed zero. That mixed-eight negative result is a release caution, not something to hide.

`bot-preflop-confirm.ts --held-out` used four fresh 2,500-hand eight-seat seeds after candidate tuning. Candidate-minus-baseline results were +436.54 and +35.99 big blinds per 100 hands against the mixed cast, and +417.59 and +541.12 against the tougher cast. All four individual intervals still crossed zero. Pooling the predeclared 10,000 paired hands gave a mean of +1,789.05 chips per hand, or +357.81 big blinds per 100, with an approximate 95% interval of +373.19 to +3,204.91 chips per hand. These are synthetic opponents and a normal-approximation interval over simulated hands, not a guarantee against people, other bot styles, or future sessions. The enormous big-blind rates also reflect fixed deep buy-ins and occasional large-pot swings; they should not be presented as realistic human poker win rates.

After the table capacity changed to nine player seats, `bot-preflop-confirm.ts --nine-seat` used four new 2,500-hand seeds. Candidate-minus-baseline results were +202.04 and +167.72 big blinds per 100 against the mixed cast, and +387.92 and +702.41 against the tougher cast. Three of four individual intervals still crossed zero. Across all 10,000 paired hands, the mean was +1,825.12 chips per hand, or +365.02 big blinds per 100, with an approximate 95% interval of +300.11 to +3,350.13 chips per hand. This supports further development under nine-seat simulation, not live promotion or a claim of expert poker. The opponents remain River's own rule and authored synthetic styles.

The benchmark resets each hand to an equal buy-in and rotates chairs. It reuses deck seeds and independent per-seat action RNG streams. Once a policy chooses a different action, subsequent public state and RNG consumption can diverge, so paired hands are not identical action histories. A discarded early audit called the baseline policy twice inside one decision wrapper, consumed extra RNG, and produced invalid negative numbers. The wrapper now calls it once, and a regression test confirms that a no-op wrapper yields identical paired outcomes. Only corrected audit results above should be cited.

## River-equity candidate: rejected on held-out outcomes

The next offline experiment added exact heads-up river enumeration. With five public cards and the actor's two private cards known, it evaluates all 990 legal opponent two-card combinations and reports tied-pot shares separately for the four existing authored starting-hand ranges. This calculation uses no opponent hole card or future state. The rule candidate only considered OG fold/call overrides on a legal, fully priced, non-all-in heads-up river; ambiguous range results kept the preflop-candidate baseline. The opponent-action predictor remains a separate, unchanged research track.

The corrected paired audit used 800 hands each for heads-up, mixed-nine and tough-nine tables on held-out `river-heldout-*` seeds. Across the three tables it observed 2,400 hands, 6,273 focal decisions, 114 river decisions facing a bet and 46 heads-up river decisions facing a bet. The candidate overrode three baseline folds, all to calls, and all three lost: 13,200, 49,813 and 22,693 chips respectively. Per-table candidate-minus-baseline changes were -3.30, -18.13 and 0 big blinds per 100 hands; every nonzero interval crossed zero. The negative calls are decisive enough to reject this rule for live use, not to claim that the baseline is optimal. An earlier audit wrapper returned the inner policy ID, causing server fallback and false chip swings despite zero recorded overrides; the wrapper was corrected to return its own ID, and the 300-hand development audit then showed exact zero differences when there were no overrides. Preserve that integrity check.

The failure is conceptual: unconditional equity against a preflop starting range is not equity *after an opponent chose to bet this river*, especially for large bets on coordinated boards. Even agreement across `random`, `loose`, `tight` and `premium` does not address this selection. The experiment's maximum measured decision time was roughly 77 ms in the held-out run; most decisions skipped enumeration, so its low overall mean is not an estimator-latency guarantee. The exact enumerator is useful as a reference and test oracle, but `rejectedRiverRangePolicy` is an explicit rejected offline policy, not a deployment candidate.

The offline benchmark now exposes a public terminal view *after* all decision hooks have run, and `collectRiverDecisionCorpus` pairs each saved pre-action heads-up river observation with a showdown result only if a call led to publicly revealed cards. Folds and unrevealed calls stay unlabelled. The later opponent hole cards never enter the pre-action observation or the returned row. Replaying the same seed reproduces the corpus; tests also force river bets, calls and folds to prove both labelled and unlabelled paths.

On a separate 800-hand seed for each of the heads-up, mixed-nine and tough-nine casts, the corpus found 83, 36 and 36 heads-up river decisions facing a bet. Only 9, 8 and 2 calls respectively received revealed-hand labels: 19 of 155 decisions overall. The other 136 were non-calls and deliberately unlabelled. This is a measured selection problem, not an excuse to infer the missing outcomes. A model trained directly on those 19 called showdowns would mostly learn from the current policy's rare willingness to call, and would have no observed correction target for the folds that matter.

The existing opponent-action forecaster may later supply a public-action prior, but its present v3 regression and shadow-only status remain unchanged; predicting an action is not predicting a winning hand.

The controlled offline scenario generator now exists. It draws distinct two-card hands and a five-card board from a seeded deck, applies one of three authored river betting rules (`value`, `balanced`, `overbluff`), and returns a legal hero observation separately from an oracle containing the opponent hole cards and true call pot share. The observation carries the public bet but no opponent hole card or style label. The rules are intentionally simple stress distributions: the value style bets two pair or better; the balanced style sometimes bluffs high card; the overbluff style also bets pairs and bluffs high card more often. They are not models of real human betting or solver ranges.

With 1,000 accepted spots per style on distinct `river-confirm-*` seeds, the hero's observed showdown shares were 0.261 against the value bettor, 0.335 against the balanced bettor and 0.474 against the overbluffer. The same 1,500-chip call into a 3,500-chip pot needs a 0.300 share to break even, so the sampled call is losing against the value style and winning against the other two. The bet and pot size were identical in all three sets, so the change came from the bettor's selected hands, not a price change. This is a sanity gate for action-conditioned models, not evidence that a live bot can identify which style is present.

## Offline learned river opponent model: useful but not deployable

The next experiment fits a small Bayesian model from seeded training examples. It learns each authored style's betting frequency and its bet likelihood for high card, pair and stronger hands. During an evaluated hand it enumerates the 990 possible opponent holdings consistent with the actor's two cards and the five public cards, weights each by the learned likelihood of the observed bet, and updates style weights only from earlier public bet/check counts. The previous hands' true cards and the current opponent's cards remain in a separate training/evaluation oracle; neither enters the policy observation. Synthetic earlier opportunities use the exact seven-card category counts recorded by [OEIS A002879](https://oeis.org/A002879) rather than repeatedly evaluating thousands of random decks. This reduced the scenario test from a five-second timeout to under a second without lowering its sample size. The current-bet evidence check also refuses an inconsistent price, missing opponent, absent or incorrect river aggressor, or multiple river raises. No learned model is wired to the live bot.

On 1,200 held-out synthetic spots, 400 each from the three trained styles, the 24-opportunity public-history version improved mean squared showdown-share error from 0.13167 to 0.11232 and call/fold payoff from 741.67 to 844.58 chips per spot against the same frozen model without history. The paired mean improvement was 102.92 chips per spot, with an approximate 95% interval of 55.94 to 149.89. The value-only slice gained 208.75; the balanced slice gained 7.50 with an interval spanning zero; the overbluff slice gained 92.50 with an interval narrowly spanning zero. The known-style oracle scored 852.08 chips per spot, but its style label is unavailable at decision time and is only an upper-bound comparison under these three assumptions. These are fixed-size, heads-up, single-river-bet simulation results, not a human win rate or a full-game profit claim. The styles were authored, not learned from real people.

Distribution shift blocks promotion. On 600 spots from an untrained polarized style, adaptive history reduced mean payoff from 1,154.17 to 556.67 chips per spot: -597.50, approximate interval -719.31 to -475.69. In a separate 200-spot learning curve for this style, mean payoff fell from 1,105 with no history to 560 after 24 prior public opportunities. More experience made the wrong model more confident. A harder untrained camouflaged style bets about as often as the trained balanced style but bluffs far more often conditional on betting. Frequency-matched training examples verify that similarity and difference. On 600 fresh camouflaged spots, adaptive history reduced payoff from 1,073.33 to 717.50: -355.83, approximate interval -460.57 to -251.10. A simple betting-frequency outlier detector cannot reliably solve this case because the public frequency is deliberately matched. Observed bet/check counts do not identify the bettor's hidden hand composition.

A separate offline reveal experiment let the model also update from categories of *earlier, publicly shown bets*. It adds the conditional evidence of a revealed hand to the existing public bet count; the current opponent's hand remains hidden. On 300 fresh spots per style, exposing a random quarter of earlier bets gave the camouflaged slice an average 2.44 shown hands and a 73.33-chip payoff gain over count-only, but its approximate interval was -15.29 to 161.96. Exposing every prior bet gave a 263.33-chip gain (147.06 to 379.61), an unrealistic best case. Value-only and overbluff decisions did not change, and the balanced slice had no quarter-reveal gain. The model still cannot represent the camouflaged style itself; reveals merely pull its weights among three familiar styles.

The random-reveal assumption is fragile. In an alternate synthetic history with strong hands more likely to be shown than bluffs, the camouflaged slice averaged 2.66 shown bets but the reveal update changed payoff by -16.67 chips relative to count-only and raised mean squared error from 0.19000 to 0.19115. Its payoff interval spans zero (-79.58 to 46.25), so this is evidence of no reliable improvement under that stress, not proof of universal harm. Real showdowns are selected by both players' decisions and may be mucked; this authored bias is only a stress test, not a fitted human reveal mechanism. Do not treat a handful of observed strong showdowns as a calibrated estimate of an opponent's entire betting range.

## Offline bet-size conditioning: informative, then misleading under shift

A separate size-aware model fits how often the three trained styles bet small (0.35 pot), medium (0.75 pot), or large (1.25 pot) with high card, pair or a stronger hand. A legal public river action supplies the current committed amount and pot before the bet. The evaluator again tests all 990 public-card-compatible opponent holdings, weighting each by the *learned probability of that size bet* rather than treating all bets alike. It never reads the current opponent's hole cards. Training examples and the scenario oracle remain separate. An optional prior-history extension records only counts of public small, medium and large bets and checks that those counts sum to the public bet count. No sized model or history read is imported by the live policy.

On 250 held-out sized spots per style, current-size conditioning versus the same fitted size-blind model and the same 24 public bet/check opportunities produced these paired payoff changes in chips per spot: value -16.8 (approximate 95% interval -39.25 to 5.65); balanced +44 (-13.20 to 101.20); overbluff +103.6 (13.83 to 193.37); unseen camouflaged +202.8 (95.23 to 310.37); unseen polarized +227.2 (130.24 to 324.16). These gains are **not** human poker evidence: the synthetic size rules explicitly make bluff and value sizes informative. The value and balanced decision intervals include zero.

Two further unseen styles tested the assumption after the first five-style result was inspected, so this is exploratory stress evidence rather than an untouched preregistered confirmation. A size-camouflaged player has approximately the balanced player's public bet rate and overall size distribution but more bluffs inside that distribution; current-size conditioning gained 62 chips per spot, with an interval of -15.88 to 139.88, while mean squared error worsened from 0.17026 to 0.17829. A reverse-sizing player puts more value into small bets and bluffs into large bets. Current-size conditioning lost 14 chips per spot (-26.17 to -1.83) and worsened mean squared error from 0.10626 to 0.12098. The size signal is real for the authored styles, not universal poker knowledge.

Adding 24 prior public opportunities with exact small/medium/large counts did not pass the next gate. Relative to the current-size model with only prior bet/check counts, historical sizes changed payoff by +6 for value, +14 for balanced, zero for overbluff, -14 for camouflaged, -2.8 for size-camouflaged, and -6 for reverse-sizing; each of those intervals includes zero. For polarized, historical sizes **reduced** payoff by 103.6 chips per spot, interval -170.65 to -36.55, and raised mean squared error from 0.26039 to 0.26880. The comparison reran the same seeded synthetic spots as above, 250 per style, with paired normal-approximation intervals; it is not a fresh confirmation. This is another direct warning that accumulating public experience under a closed three-style model can make River more certain and less correct.

## Accepted-play history availability

The offline benchmark now has a read-only collector for a player's first accepted heads-up river check or opening bet in each hand. It classifies the public committed amount against the pre-bet pot and adds a hand category only if the terminal *public* room view actually shows that bettor's cards. It retains no private hole cards or full private bot observation, rejects duplicate opportunities within a hand, excludes the current/future hand from a prior-history snapshot, and does not change the live bot or persistence path. Fixed bettor/caller and bettor/folder tests prove that a called, publicly shown bet can be labelled while a fold cannot.

A 300-hand ordinary-rule audit found 168 qualifying opportunities, 9 bets and 3 publicly shown bets across a heads-up table. A nine-seat table found only 74 qualifying opportunities, 7 bets and 3 publicly shown bets across all nine players; individuals had 3–13 opportunities each. These are two seeded synthetic casts, not an estimate of human traffic. Crucially, they show that the earlier experiment's 24 prior river opportunities *per opponent* is not representative of a typical short nine-seat session here. Strong within-session learning from only this river signal is therefore data-starved; persistent encounters, broader public street/action context, and a conservative cold-start are necessary. Do not present the 24-opportunity gain as current live-bot behaviour.

Release gate: retain the rule-based live bot and keep all these river models offline. A further candidate needs an explicit unknown-style or uncertainty fallback, a defensible treatment of selectively revealed hands, and a public-history feature collection path from actual accepted play. It must pass fresh, predeclared nine-seat and heads-up hands, the untrained shift styles, the old large-bet failures, legal-action and decision-time gates, and a leak test that proves no unrevealed card reaches inference. Bet sizing alone is not a guarantee against a player who imitates sizes or reverses their meaning; when strategies are observationally indistinguishable, the correct behaviour is uncertainty rather than an invented expert read. The separately rejected opponent-action forecaster remains separate and shadow-only.

## Standing release boundary

- Keep the narrow guard as the live default; keep the broad preflop candidate explicit and offline. No cloud flag, Supabase migration, trained-model rollout or client protocol change is part of this pass.
- Expand the scenario corpus to price short-stack calls, multiway pots, stronger opponent ranges, domination and reverse implied odds. The ordinal score is especially weak as an input to a formula that subtracts pot odds as though the score were equity.
- Establish a real preflop range/equity baseline before calling OG expert. Measure against multiple stronger external or authored opponents, not only River's own rule bots. Stratify by position, stack depth and hand class; retain losing slices instead of selecting only aggregate gains.
- Keep opponent-action ML evaluation separate from strategic policy evaluation. The current v3 predictor is rejected for ordinary-opponent regression, and none of these preflop benchmarks changes that result. A live learned policy needs held-out wins without leaks, calibration on legal pre-action data, latency and legal-action gates, and an explicit rollout decision.
