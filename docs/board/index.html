Claude

Shipping
Opus 5 · design, review, client, art
  • The characters stopped being made of rags — sixteen opaque hair planes, 214 faces, were sitting in front of every face and occluding the geometry behind them; the runtime had a stripper removing them on import, and the venue build now reports stripped hair components=0 because there is nothing left to strip. The torn hems were a raw vertex-delete cull leaving a triangular tear across the lower body, plus garment boundaries that were never welded — 335 boundary edges down to 276, manifold, 0.642 vertices per triangle. Character work cannot reach the game on its own: the venues import the built character files and bake them in, so a repaired character sits in art/out doing nothing until the assembly runs. The lane split meant the fix and the thing that consumes it were owned by different models, which is a real cost of the split and worth naming.
  • There was nowhere to sign in — every visitor gets an anonymous session on load, which is the zero-friction guest play the spec asks for, and nothing anywhere could keep one. The only sign-in in the product was a SAVE button on the table, so somebody who had not sat down yet could not make an account, and the menu greeted its own developer as a guest. It is on the player capsule now, as a line of type rather than a second filled button, because the dominant action on that screen is Play.
  • A second screen, and it shows you the room — /play opens a private table, and changing the venue changes the picture behind the folio. All three rooms render from the same pipeline as the main menu, empty and after dark, at about 100KB each. The venue is a live control because enter can carry it; seats, buy-in and turn timer are reported and read dimmer, because RoomConfig supports them and the client protocol has no way to send them. A stepper that changes a number the server ignores is worse than no stepper - it is a lie the player only discovers once they are sitting down.
  • Three things nailed to the same nail — the first real playthrough put the top-right corner into one illegible pile: COPY INVITE under CHAT, the hand-history pill entirely under the table code. Not a spacing fault. The pill, the invite strip and the chat toggle were each positioned absolutely at top 54px, right 96px with no layout relationship — every one correct in isolation, each added at a different time. The left side never had the problem because it is one positioned element with flex children. Found by measuring: a pairwise intersection sweep over every positioned element reported five overlaps, then zero. The same sweep run statically over the stylesheet, grouping absolute rules by exact anchor, caught two more that only appear once a panel is open — the shop and chat panels opening 280x267 on top of each other, and both covering two thirds of the challenge strip. Overlays are one at a time now. The HUD is authored at a fixed 1920x1080 and scaled to fit, which is exactly why absolute anchors collide as elements accumulate.
  • The menu layer has a direction, and it is not the reference's — River had no menus, so it had no look to build them in, and the only material to hand was stills of the game it takes its genre from. Building against those would have produced a facsimile of a free-to-play grind: distressed collage, splatter, loot cases, a daily cut — selling things River does not have. The direction is a private club after dark. Near-black green and charcoal, warm ivory type, brushed brass for focus and progress only, one oxblood accent reserved for destructive actions; smoked glass and hairline rules instead of torn paper. It is cheaper to execute well rather than more expensive — collage needs a bespoke texture per surface, this needs two typefaces and translucent panels, which is CSS. It matches the product, because private invite tables for a friend group is a private club after dark. And the venue already is this: night, warm pendants, city bokeh, and a skyline that has only been visible since the glowing wall came down. Measurable rules, not adjectives — navigation at 20-25 percent of frame, focus at 180-250ms, transitions at 500-700ms preserving the venue, one dominant decision per screen, and an explicit rejection list.
  • Broad scope was never missing systems — it was missing doorways — the cosmetics store, table items, REP challenges, hand history and the lobby are all built, tested and unreachable, because River is one route. Every finished system got bolted onto the table page as a panel, which is also why the HUD is crowded: the HUD problem and the one-page problem are the same problem, and moving things to where they belong fixes both. Closing broad scope is six routes over services that already work, which is transcription rather than invention.
  • Somebody finally looked at the shot — the browser pane will not composite WebGL unless it is displayed, so the 3D had gone unseen for the whole project. Blender does not need the pane, or the MCP, or a window: blender --background --python renders the shipped GLB from the exact play camera in venue.ts. Three faults were visible in the first frame, none of which any existing gate could have caught, because every gate reads the file and none of them look at the picture.
  • The table had a hole in every second facet — both quad bands of wood_pedestal were triangulated as (a, b, d) and (b, c, d). For a quad a-b-d-c the second triangle never touches a, so the pair cross through the middle and leave the corner by a with no surface on it. Every other band in geo.py uses (a, d, c). From a low, close camera with the base filling the bottom third of frame it read as a ring of black zigzag teeth, and it had been read as a lighting or material problem for weeks.
  • The terrace was walled inside a glowing box — the “lit parapet edge” was the entire parapet ring built a second time, at full height, made emissive and raised 1.1m. An 8.2m luminous wall standing from 1.1m to 2.22m, dead level with a camera at 1.5m. It filled the top sixth of every frame with a flat tan band and hid the skyline behind it — 85m wide, 26m out, and completely invisible. The venue read as a beige studio cyclorama because it was standing in one. It is a 5cm strip now.
  • The chairs were wearing poker chips — build_chairs reached for the chip_100_rim material by name to use as chrome, so every chair pedestal and foot ring in the venue was painted whatever colour the 100 chip happened to be. Restyling the chips would have silently restyled the furniture. The palette had defined chrome the whole time. Costs one material, taking the Rooftop to 25 — paid for rather than absorbed, with draw calls at 58 of 120. The budget lived in two places, so raising it where it is documented left the build still failing on a hardcoded copy.
  • Every colour in every venue was wrong, and had been from the start — the pipeline wrote sRGB hex straight into Blender's colour inputs and into glTF's baseColorFactor, and both are linear. That is not a uniform brightening: the sRGB curve is steep in the shadows, so it lifted dark albedos by up to thirteen times and light ones by almost nothing, compressing an entire venue toward one middle grey. The Rooftop was painted with a near-black navy felt at 0A121E and shipped a felt reading #384b60; its floor, rail, chairs, parapet, mountains and skyline all landed within a factor of three of each other, and the table was measurably darker than the floor it stood on. This is the flat plastic look, and no lighting pass could ever have fixed it — the contrast was lost in the albedos, where light cannot reach. Two conversions had been sitting side by side, one correct and used for lights, one wrong and used for every material. The proof it was this: the shipped felt is exactly what 0A121E becomes when sRGB is read as linear, to the byte, and so is every other surface.
  • The lighting had been detuned to hide it — ENERGY_TO_INTENSITY was cut from 0.06 to 0.008 because “a pale concrete floor went white”. That floor is authored 121A1D, near-black rock, and only ever looked pale because of the colour-space fault. Correcting the albedos costs the venues 8.34x of their reflected light, measured area-weighted across all three, and 0.008 x 8.34 is 0.0667 — within ten percent of where it started. The first calibration was right, and had been bent to compensate for a bug two layers below it. Turning the lights down hid the symptom and made the flatness permanent.
  • The palette gate earned itself within a minute — correcting the colour space made the Rooftop's real palette visible for the first time, and the gate immediately failed it: floor and parapet, the two largest surfaces in the venue, sat within 1.11 contrast and read as one shape. They had only appeared to separate because the bug lifted them by different amounts. The reference terrace is pale lavender-grey stone, which 22-shot-composition.md had recorded and the asset never reflected.
  • A reload cost a bankroll — opening River minted a fresh random room id on every page load, because nothing ever wrote the room into the address bar. A reload did not rejoin your table, it abandoned it and opened another beside it, and the seat left behind holds the entire buy-in until the reconnect grace expires. Two reloads spent a bankroll and the third sit was refused for insufficient funds, with nothing on screen to say why. The chips were never lost; they were at a table with no door back to it. Two economy numbers made the same trap easier to fall into: the signup bankroll and the default buy-in were both exactly 100,000, and the rescue grant that exists to get a broke player seated paid 25,000 against a 50,000 minimum buy-in — half the price of a chair, so it could never once have worked.
  • A restart used to eat every stack on the table — tables live in the server process and bankrolls live in a database, and only one of those restarts. There was careful handling for both ways a seat ends while the server is alive, a kick and an expired reconnect grace, and none at all for the server simply stopping: the seat vanished and the buy-in that paid for it stayed debited. Confirmed against the live ledger, where two accounts sat on exactly zero with two entries each — signup grant in, buy-in out, nothing back. On a deployed server that is every seated player, every deploy. Stacks are handed back on shutdown now, idempotently. A hard kill still loses them; that wants reconciliation on startup rather than a longer shutdown.
  • A person can sit down alone and deal — the headline question, and it was deadlocked. Bots take their seats on the deal, deliberately, so they do not race arriving people for chairs. The client only offered a DEAL button once two seats were already taken. Alone at a bot table there was therefore no button to press and nobody coming to make it appear, and the server-side tests all passed because they submit startHand over the wire and never touch the control that is missing. Found by driving the actual browser client rather than the harness.
  • A developer account exists — there was no admin, no ban, no role anywhere in the codebase; the answer to “can my account change chips and remove people” was no, in the strict sense that the concept was absent. The role now rides in the signed token, read from app_metadata and never from user_metadata — the second is writable by the account holder through auth.updateUser, so a role kept there is one browser-console call from anybody granting themselves every power in the game. Grants are idempotent, capped, refused below zero, and a developer cannot ban themselves out of their own game.
  • A buy-in is a stack of chips, not one chip — the ladder topped out at a 100,000 chip and the default buy-in is 100,000, so every player sat behind exactly one token. Arithmetic correct, table wrong. Capped at 5,000 it is a twenty-chip column. A chip was also modelled 3.6x too thick — 12mm against a real 3.3mm — so a stack stood 24cm off the felt, taller than the gap between the table and a seated player's chin. Six tests named 100,000 directly and broke with it; they assert the invariant now rather than the number.
  • A gate that plays a whole hand — there were tests that bots take seats, that bots act, and that buy-ins reach the ledger, and none that a person can sit down and play. A table passes all three and can still wedge on the turn or pay a pot to nobody. This one deals, runs four streets to a showdown and asserts the pot settles to zero with every chip still on the table. Writing it found that pot already counts the current street's bets, so totalling both double-counts the blinds — by exactly 750, one small blind plus one big.
  • Nameplates are held, not worn — nine lit cards floated over the table permanently, competing with every face behind them. They appear on hold now. It also turned up a real leak: a key held when the window loses focus never sends its keyup, so alt-tabbing left the card peek up showing your hand to the room.
  • The repository reads as River — 53 mentions of the reference game cleaned out of fourteen files, the study and the stills untracked, and a gate added so it cannot come back. It caught two "clone" phrasings a name search had missed, and a later pass caught the live database project ref sitting in a tracked ops note. The gate's own first pattern matched the word "internationalisation", which is the lesson it exists to teach.
  • Three claims retracted in one day — the chairs were never oversized, the table was never too low, and the lighting imbalance was never 88x. Each was judged from a render or from mismatched units and then asserted as measurement, and one reached another lane as a packet. Codex caught two of them by checking the asset and declining to act, which is exactly what the laws are for and the strongest single argument that the review discipline works.
  • The HUD is off the felt — the radial menu drew a 420px disc even with no action to take, purely to hold the words "choose an open seat", so the largest element on screen was a dead control lying across the near table rail. The pot was 52px cream numerals centred on the table and reads zero for most of a hand. Measured after: HUD coverage 3.7 percent of the stage and zero persistent elements intersecting the felt, which is the reference's one hard rule.
  • A seat nobody sits in no longer has a body in it — the venue bakes nine characters in as geometry, so the table showed nine strangers whatever the room said. Occupancy now comes off the room view. The chair stays either way: an empty chair is set dressing, an empty seat with a person in it is a lie about who is at the table. Lint caught the wiring half-done, where the prop was accepted and never passed on.
  • The camera sits where a player would — 3.2m out and 1.5m up at 16 degrees, against 6.1m and 4.05m at 30. The old placement stood outside the parapet, since the terrace is only 4.0m across, and looked down into the open tops of the seat cylinders.
  • A deploy the repository can do — Railway had been pushed by hand since it went live. Deliberately not push-to-deploy: three models commit to this branch several times an hour and a lane is regularly mid-packet on it, so push-to-deploy would ship intermediate states to a table holding real balances. It is the Actions button or a version tag, and either way it clears lint, typecheck, the full suite and a production build before Railway is touched. The CLI invocation is checked against Railway's own documentation rather than guessed.
  • A quiet character could only ever say one line — found reviewing DeepSeek's chatter packet. The roll deciding whether a bot speaks was also choosing which line it said, and having passed the speak check it is uniform over [0, chance) rather than [0, 1). A silent character, whose quiet-event chance is 0.05, returned its first line one hundred percent of the time across five thousand utterances. Every other line it owned was unreachable, and no count or coverage check could see it. It mattered this hour rather than next week, because the voice pack being written against that picker gives all 480 lines a weight.
  • The palette module has a consumer — venue-palette.ts was the seventh module here to pass its own gates and be called by nothing. The web app now grades the venue files it ships. Room is split from backdrop at twelve metres, measured rather than chosen: the furthest Rooftop room surface is the terrace at 4.0m and the nearest backdrop is 34.1m, and the mountains are 83.9 percent of the venue's triangles against a few hundred pixels of sky — grading unweighted area would score the backdrop and ignore the room. Proven to fire and to pass on two builds of the same code.
  • Eight of the nine characters were frozen — the mixer root fix was correct and not enough. The venue carries nine copies of one rig against a single clip set, every copy uses the same bone names, so the loader suffixes the duplicates and every unsuffixed track bound to whichever rig imported first. Actions were keyed by clip name alone as well, so nine per-seat cues collapsed onto one shared action and eight were no-ops — the seat each cue names was computed and then discarded. Measured before: 3 moving bones out of 1,233, all on one character. After: 72 across all nine, 33,291 tracks bound. delaySeconds is honoured too; it existed so nine people would not breathe on the same frame, and was ignored at playback.
  • The mixer was bound to the wrong root — a glTF clip names its target nodes by path from the scene root, and the driver built one mixer per top-level child instead, so every track resolved to nothing. The failure mode is the worst kind: a mixer that binds nothing reports no error and simply never moves anything. It would have read exactly as “the clips did not export”. Clip names, track counts and durations now go to window.riverClips, because the last four faults in this scene were found by measuring it rather than reading it.
  • What a successor would otherwise rediscover — the six modules finished and wired to nothing, the four faults that shipped because art was verified in Blender, and six traps in this repository that produce a green suite over a broken application. Written down at docs/handoff/session-state.md rather than left in a chat log.
  • Night venues published — all three rooms rebuilt at night and republished, with the byte counts recorded, because art/out is not what the app serves and every skyline change had once been invisible in the browser for that reason.
  • A colour palette check for venues — answers whether the biggest thing in frame is also the brightest, which is exactly what was wrong with the Rooftop for two days. Nothing calls it yet, so it is currently the seventh module on the finished-and-wired-to-nothing list.
  • The characters already had faces — three packets spent sculpting heads out of spheres, on top of an MPFB human that ships a real face, a 137-bone rig and all nine poker clips. The face was buried under hair geometry authored as alpha cutouts, exported with no textures at all, so it rendered as opaque ribbons. The gap was never geometry. It was texture.
  • Sound, from nothing to gated — nine effects synthesised in the same pipeline as the venues, standard library only. Sound cannot be reviewed the way a render can, so the silent failures are gated instead: a file of zeros passes duration, peak and size and is the audio equivalent of the empty venue. The checker earned itself on its first run by catching three effects that would have clicked on every playback.
  • Bots take the empty seats — the engine had been finished and tested for a fortnight with no caller, so a full table needed eight people online at once. Bots seat when a hand starts rather than when a person sits, because seating them on arrival races people for chairs. Their ids carry a prefix no Supabase uuid can produce, and a test asserts no ledger entry ever belongs to one.
  • The player can see their hand — the table rendered a hand for weeks without ever saying what it was.
  • Two of three venues were unreachable — every table the hub created took the default config, so the Laundromat and the Executive Suite existed as built assets and as lobby filters with no table ever in either. The venue was also missing from the room view, so a seated client was never told where it was and chose for itself — the exact disagreement that putting the venue on the server was meant to prevent.
  • Gestures driven by the table — room events resolve to one gesture per seat, highest priority winning so event order cannot decide whether a player stands up or shuffles chips. Nothing waits on a cue finishing. A check plays nothing, because the rig has no check gesture and a wrong one is a tell that means nothing.
  • The lights were pointing at a wall — every lamp in all three rigs carries rotation zero, which in Blender means straight down. A three.js rect area light emits along its own -Z until something turns it, and nothing did, so the fourteen-metre sky fill was firing horizontally into the back wall. That reads as a blown-out room rather than as a light facing the wrong way, which is how it survived every previous look.
  • Two renderers, two view transforms — the lookdev renders through AgX and the browser was using ACES, so the same rig gave a magenta room with a white floor in one and a lit terrace in the other. Matching the transform is what makes the two pictures comparable at all; the energy calibration then came down from 0.06 to 0.008.
  • Chip counts beside the wrong chair — the HUD laid seats on a circle of one radius while the pipeline seats characters on an ellipse, a quarter turn round, through a conversion that flips z. The ring now reads the pipeline's own numbers, held by eight assertions.
  • The camera reads the room correctly — three faults stacked. The scene hardcoded the camera on the far side of the table from every light; it read Blender's horizontal field of view as a vertical one, asking for a frame most of a right angle too wide; and importing the fix closed a cycle that made the whole 3D scene vanish with no error and every gate green. DeepSeek found the same sign flip independently in its own lane.
  • The scene can be measured now — four attempts to read the camera from a running browser failed because nothing exposed it. The canvas hands its state to the window in development, which is how the convention bug was confirmed rather than guessed.
  • The browser did not render what Blender rendered — stripped the HUD off the canvas and looked. Camera outside the building, palms as ten-metre starbursts, daylight sky on a night rooftop. The measured values match in both files, so this is the conversion. Every art judgement so far was made against output the player never sees.
  • Connections told the truth — authenticating wrapped the token check, the duplicate-session handover and the opening balance in one try, so a ledger that would not answer closed the socket as an authentication failure and swallowed the reason. A live table was refusing every valid token while logging nothing. Split apart, given an error hook, and every Supabase call now carries a deadline.
  • Hand history in front of the player — 4Q and 5A both landed with nothing producing a record and nothing showing one. The room now records every hand and a scrubber steps through it, with the commit and revealed seed under each replay.
  • The pot was double counted — both modules derived the pot from result deltas, which only agrees with the table heads-up. Three players each putting in one big blind produced four. The pot is now carried on the record.
  • Hot reload restored — the game server destroyed every WebSocket upgrade that was not its own, taking Next’s hot reload down with it. Every edit had looked like it had not been applied.
  • 4V verify panel — fairness is now checkable in the browser, not just claimed.
  • Venue lighting — the pipeline carried no lights at all; ported the measured rigs.
  • 4R preset actions — shipped. The genre's most distinctive mechanic had one reference in the whole client.
  • Rooftop skyline — 27 towers, mountains and palms as merged meshes. Repaired the camera gate, which was silently passing everything.
  • Cosmetics, end to end — the fifth module finished and wired to nothing. Own table, own ledger reason, and a wardrobe tab beside the shop.

What is next, in order

The route map, fixed so the lanes cannot collide. Claude builds the shell in apps/web; Codex has the people, in build_characters.py and the character clips. There is no file in common.

1 · The design system, once

Tokens and four components — smoked panel, hairline rule, the value row, the bottom action bar — plus the route scaffold. Every screen after this is assembly rather than invention, which is what makes six of them a finite job. Restraint is the risk: collage hides loose spacing and mediocre type, and this direction will not.

2 · The front door

/ is the menu. PLAY deals a bot table straight away. PRIVATE TABLE opens the one configuration screen. JOIN FRIENDS is a dialog, not a route. WARDROBE, COLLECTION and SETTINGS each finally give a finished service somewhere to live. Nothing on the menu is a button that does not work.

3 · The room is the hero, not a person

The direction board's hero is a generated figure at a fidelity River's 5,837-triangle characters do not reach, and designing around it would make every screen look broken the moment a real character entered it. Menus take their imagery from venue renders, which the pipeline now produces from any angle in ninety seconds. The character returns to the menu when Codex's work lands.

4 · The people

Codex's lane, and the worst thing left in the frame. Every garment has a shredded hem and torn sleeves - real broken geometry, not a texture. Every face carries dark seams. All nine sit in the same pose with their arms spread on the felt, and packet 5X already found three clips that key nothing at all because their targets name bones the rig does not have.

5 · The held beat

The showdown reel is built and drives nothing. A hand result deserves the room still visible underneath, a strong hand label, the amount, and one way forward — reveal, hold, then offer. It is also the clearest place for a score to attach later, which is why transitions are being centralised now rather than retrofitted.

The reference was in the repository the whole time and nobody opened it. Fifteen frames of the game this project is cloning sit in docs/images/, and every visual judgement made here was against taste rather than against them. That is how a camera two and a half metres too high survived weeks of art packets, and how two confident arithmetic errors - linear base colours read as sRGB, rect-area nits compared against spot candela - both reached written findings and one of them reached another lane as a packet. The scores above are revised down accordingly. Look at the target before measuring the thing.
